# Chapter 7: US/Texas Constitution, Bill of Rights, and the Criminal Justice System — Question Bank

*This bank covers BPOC Chapter 7 (LOs 7.1–7.6): the US Constitution and Bill of Rights, the
corresponding Texas Constitution Article 1 (Bill of Rights) provisions, the related Code of
Criminal Procedure Chapter 1 rights articles, the structure of the criminal justice system, and
the civil/criminal court system. A number of questions specifically test the differences between
the US and Texas versions of the same right (e.g., habeas corpus suspension standards, bail
guarantees, jury composition) — read carefully, since the two documents don't always say the
same thing even when BPOC groups them under the same heading.*

### Q2 — Concept
**LO:** 7.1 · **Source:** TX Constitution Art. 1 Sec. 12; CCP Art. 1.08

Under the **Texas Constitution**, when can the writ of habeas corpus be suspended?

- [ ] During a declared state of emergency
- [ ] During martial law only
- [ ] In cases of rebellion or invasion, if public safety requires it
- [x] Never — the Texas Constitution states it "shall never be suspended," with no exception listed

**Explanation:** TX Const. Art. 1 Sec. 12 says only: "The writ of Habeas Corpus is a writ of
right, and shall never be suspended. The Legislature shall enact laws to render the remedy
speedy and effectual." No rebellion/invasion exception appears in the Texas text — that
exception is a **federal** concept. CCP Art. 1.08 repeats the same absolute "shall never be
suspended" language.

### Q3 — Concept
**LO:** 7.1 · **Source:** USC Art. 1 Sec. 9 Cl. 2; TX Constitution Art. 1 Sec. 12

This is the classic US-vs-Texas comparison trap. Which statement correctly describes the
difference between the two constitutions on suspending habeas corpus?

- [ ] Both documents allow suspension only during rebellion or invasion
- [ ] Both documents say the writ can never be suspended under any circumstance
- [x] The US Constitution allows suspension "in Cases of Rebellion or Invasion" if public safety requires it; the Texas Constitution contains no such exception and says the writ "shall never be suspended"
- [ ] The Texas Constitution allows suspension during rebellion, while the US Constitution allows no suspension at all

**Explanation:** US Const. Art. 1 Sec. 9 Cl. 2: "The Privilege of the Writ of Habeas Corpus shall
not be suspended, unless when in Cases of Rebellion or Invasion the public Safety may require
it." That's a conditional guarantee with a built-in exception. TX Const. Art. 1 Sec. 12 has no
such exception — its language is unconditional. A Texas peace officer relying on "the
Constitution" needs to know which one they mean, since the state document is actually the
*stronger* protection of the two on this point.

### Q7 — Concept
**LO:** 7.1 · **Source:** TX Constitution Art. 1 Sec. 8

The Texas Constitution's free speech/press provision (Art. 1 Sec. 8) includes a specific
evidentiary rule that has no counterpart in the plain text of the federal First Amendment. What
is it?

- [ ] It requires a warrant before seizing a printing press
- [x] In libel prosecutions for publishing about public officers or matters of public concern, the truth of the published matter may be given in evidence, and the jury decides both the law and the facts
- [ ] It bans prior restraint on all speech without exception
- [ ] It requires unanimous jury verdicts in libel cases

**Explanation:** TX Const. Art. 1 Sec. 8: "In prosecutions for the publication of papers,
investigating the conduct of officers, or men in public capacity, or when the matter published
is proper for public information, the truth thereof may be given in evidence. And in all
indictments for libels, the jury shall have the right to determine the law and the facts..." The
federal First Amendment's text is a bare prohibition on Congress abridging speech/press — it
doesn't spell out a truth defense or a law-and-facts jury rule itself (those developed through
case law and separate state provisions like this one).

### Q10 — Concept
**LO:** 7.1 · **Source:** USC Amendment I; TX Constitution Art. 1 Sec. 8

Structurally, the First Amendment is written as a limit on Congress ("Congress shall make no
law..."), while TX Const. Art. 1 Sec. 8 is written as an affirmative individual right ("Every
person shall be at liberty to speak..."). Does this textual difference mean the federal First
Amendment doesn't restrict Texas state and local government action?

- [ ] Correct — only Congress is bound by the First Amendment; states are free to restrict speech as they wish
- [x] No — despite the literal "Congress" text, the First Amendment has long been held to apply to state and local governments too (through the Fourteenth Amendment's due process clause), so both documents restrict Texas actors; the wording difference is textual/structural, not a live gap in protection
- [ ] Only the Texas provision applies to state actors; the First Amendment applies only to federal officers
- [ ] The two provisions are worded identically and neither is broader than the other

**Explanation:** This tests whether students notice the literal wording of Amendment I without
overreading it — it says "Congress," but incorporation doctrine has long applied core Bill of
Rights protections to the states via the Fourteenth Amendment. For a Texas peace officer, both
the state and federal free-speech guarantees are live restrictions on official conduct.

### Q11 — Recall
**LO:** 7.1 · **Source:** USC Amendment II; TX Constitution Art. 1 Sec. 23

The Second Amendment protects the right to keep and bear arms tied to "a well regulated
Militia." How does TX Constitution Art. 1 Sec. 23 frame the same right?

- [ ] Identically — it also conditions the right entirely on militia service
- [x] As a right of every citizen to keep and bear arms in lawful defense of himself or the State, subject to legislative regulation "with a view to prevent crime"
- [ ] It doesn't address firearms at all
- [ ] It limits the right to law enforcement officers only

**Explanation:** TX Const. Art. 1 Sec. 23: "Every citizen shall have the right to keep and bear

amendment's militia-clause framing, the Texas provision frames it as an individual right from
the outset, paired with an explicit legislative regulation power.

### Q18 — Concept
**LO:** 7.1 · **Source:** USC Amendment V; TX Constitution Art. 1 Sec. 14

Both the US and Texas double jeopardy clauses bar being tried twice for the same offense — but
they use different words for the underlying interest at stake. Which pairing is accurate?

- [x] The Fifth Amendment says a person shall not "be subject for the same offence to be twice put in jeopardy of life or limb"; TX Const. Art. 1 Sec. 14 says "twice put in jeopardy of life or liberty" — different phrasing ("limb" vs. "liberty"), same modern scope (both bar double prosecution for the same offense, not just death-or-dismemberment cases)
- [ ] The federal clause only applies to capital cases; the Texas clause applies to all criminal cases
- [ ] The Texas clause has been repealed and Texans rely solely on the federal Fifth Amendment
- [ ] The two clauses are worded identically, word for word

**Explanation:** The federal text's archaic "life or limb" phrasing survives from 18th-century
drafting but has long been read to cover ordinary criminal jeopardy generally, not literal
death or dismemberment. Texas's "life or liberty" wording is more modern-sounding but reaches
the same substantive ground. A student who reads "life or limb" literally and assumes it's
narrower than the Texas version would be wrong — they're understood to protect the same scope.

### Q19 — Concept
**LO:** 7.1 · **Source:** USC Amendment V; TX Constitution Art. 1 Sec. 10; CCP Art. 1.05

The Fifth Amendment requires grand jury indictment for "capital, or otherwise infamous crime,"
except for cases arising in the land/naval forces or militia in actual service during war or
public danger. How does TX Constitution Art. 1 Sec. 10's indictment requirement compare?

- [ ] It's identical — same two exceptions, word for word
- [x] Texas's exceptions are broader: no indictment is required when punishment is by fine or imprisonment other than in the penitentiary (i.e., most misdemeanors), in cases of impeachment, or in military cases during war/public danger — meaning Texas can prosecute misdemeanors by information without ever convening a grand jury
- [ ] Texas requires grand jury indictment for every criminal case, with no exceptions at all
- [ ] The federal exception list is broader than the Texas one

**Explanation:** TX Const. Art. 1 Sec. 10's indictment requirement excludes cases "in which the
punishment is by fine or imprisonment, otherwise than in the penitentiary" — essentially all but
the most serious offenses — plus impeachment and military cases. CCP Art. 1.05 boils this down
practically: "No person shall be held to answer for a felony unless on indictment of a grand
jury," implicitly confirming misdemeanors don't require one.

- [x] "Due course of law"

### Q36 — Concept
**LO:** 7.1 · **Source:** USC Amendment VIII; TX Constitution Art. 1 Sec. 11; CCP Art. 1.07

Here's a genuinely tricky distinction: does the federal Eighth Amendment, by itself, guarantee
that a criminal defendant is *entitled* to be released on bail?

Art. 1 Sec. 11 affirmatively makes "all prisoners" bailable by sufficient sureties (except capital offenses where the proof is evident), and CCP Art. 1.07 states any person is eligible for bail unless denial is expressly permitted by the Texas Constitution or other law
- [ ] Yes — the Eighth Amendment explicitly creates a federal constitutional right to bail in every case
