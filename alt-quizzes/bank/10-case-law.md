Officers lawfully knock on an apartment door and identify themselves as police. They then hear what sounds like people scrambling to flush evidence down a toilet. Believing evidence is being destroyed, they force entry without a warrant. Under Kentucky v. King, is this warrantless entry valid?

- [x] Yes — because the officers' knock-and-announce was itself lawful and did not threaten to violate the Fourth Amendment, the occupants' own reaction created a genuine exigency (risk of evidence destruction) that the officers may lawfully act on
- [ ] No — any exigency that arises after police knock on a door is automatically police-created and invalid
- [ ] No — officers must always obtain a warrant even when they reasonably believe evidence is actively being destroyed
- [ ] Yes, but only if the officers first obtained verbal consent from someone inside

**Explanation:** This tracks King's facts and holding directly: a lawful knock-and-announce, followed by the occupants' own choice to try to destroy evidence, created a legitimate exigency that the police did not improperly manufacture.

### Q117 — Definition
**LO:** 10.8 · **Source:** Kentucky v. King, 563 U.S. 452 (2011)

What is the "police-created exigency" doctrine addressed in Kentucky v. King?

- [x] The principle that officers cannot rely on an exigency they themselves brought about through conduct that violates or threatens to violate the Fourth Amendment
- [ ] A doctrine that bars police from ever knocking on a suspect's door
- [ ] A doctrine that automatically excuses any warrant requirement whenever police are physically present at a scene
- [ ] A doctrine limited exclusively to vehicle searches

**Explanation:** The "police-created exigency" issue asks whether the exigency police are relying on was itself the product of unlawful or threatened-unlawful police conduct — if so, they cannot use it to justify a warrantless entry; if the underlying police conduct was lawful, they generally can.

### Q118 — Concept
**LO:** 10.8 · **Source:** Kentucky v. King, 563 U.S. 452 (2011)

Under King, what specifically disqualifies police from relying on an exigency they helped bring about?

- [x] The exigency is disqualified only if police created it by engaging in or threatening conduct that itself violates the Fourth Amendment — lawful investigative conduct, like a proper knock-and-announce, does not disqualify a resulting exigency
- [ ] Any police presence near the home at all, regardless of conduct, disqualifies any later exigency
- [ ] Nothing disqualifies reliance on an exigency; police may always act on any exigency regardless of its cause
- [ ] Only exigencies that arise more than 24 hours after police first arrive are disqualified

**Explanation:** The touchstone is the lawfulness of the officers' own conduct — King draws the line at whether the police violated or threatened to violate the Fourth Amendment in bringing about the exigency, not simply whether police presence contributed to it in some general sense.

### Q119 — Scenario
**LO:** 10.8 · **Source:** Kentucky v. King, 563 U.S. 452 (2011)

Suppose instead that officers, lacking probable cause or reasonable suspicion, falsely announced through a bullhorn that they had a search warrant and were about to break down the door, causing panicked occupants to attempt to destroy evidence. Officers then force entry, citing the resulting exigency. Under King's reasoning, would this exigency justify the warrantless entry?

- [x] No — because the officers' own conduct (falsely claiming to have a warrant, effectively threatening an unlawful forced entry) was itself improper, the resulting exigency would be police-created in the way King says disqualifies reliance on it
- [ ] Yes — any exigency, however it arises, always justifies warrantless entry under King
- [ ] Yes, because King held that all exigencies are per se valid once evidence destruction is suspected
- [ ] No, but only because a bullhorn was used instead of a spoken announcement

**Explanation:** This is the flip side of King's actual holding — where the officers' own conduct in bringing about the exigency is itself improper (here, falsely claiming authority to force entry), the resulting exigency cannot be relied upon, unlike the lawful knock-and-announce in the real King case.

### Q120 — Concept
**LO:** 10.8 · **Source:** Kentucky v. King, 563 U.S. 452 (2011); Brigham City v. Stuart, 547 U.S. 398 (2006)

Both King and Brigham City ultimately upheld warrantless home entries. What common analytical thread connects the two holdings?

- [x] Both rest on an objective assessment of the circumstances officers actually faced — genuine, objectively reasonable exigency (evidence destruction in King; emergency aid in Brigham City) justified entry without a warrant, without regard to the officers' subjective motives or, in King's case, without regard to the fact that police presence played some role in triggering the occupants' reaction
- [ ] Both cases turned entirely on the presence of a valid arrest warrant
- [ ] Both cases held that a warrant is always required regardless of any exigency
- [ ] Both cases apply exclusively to vehicle stops, not to homes

**Explanation:** Despite addressing different exigency categories, both cases reflect the same underlying exigent-circumstances principle: an objectively reasonable, genuinely exigent situation — not officer intent, and not the mere fact that lawful police conduct set events in motion — is what justifies dispensing with the warrant requirement.
